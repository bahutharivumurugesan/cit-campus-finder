# Project outline: CIT Campus Finder

## 1. Problem

Students, new joiners, and campus visitors may lose time locating blocks, services, or facilities when they do not know the campus layout.

## 2. Proposed solution

A mobile-friendly website where a person searches a place, sees its building/floor/landmark, and gets a short direction clue from a chosen campus starting point.

## 3. Intended users

- First-year students
- Students moving between unfamiliar blocks
- Parents and campus visitors
- Students helping visitors navigate

## 4. First version features

- Search and category filters
- Place information card
- Clickable campus overview
- Student-verified route and floor information
- Clear indication when a location is not yet verified
- Local-only suggestion form for demonstrating a correction workflow

## 5. Validation plan

Interview 5–10 students. Record the most commonly hard-to-find places. Test the prototype with 5 students using three destination-finding tasks. Measure task completion, time, and points of confusion.

## 6. Success measures

- Number of students who can find a selected place using the prototype
- Median time to locate a place in a test task
- Number of locations with confirmed directions
- Student feedback on clarity and usefulness

## 7. Current scope and limitations

The starter is a static web prototype. It has no GPS, indoor positioning, live navigation, login, or database. Its campus illustration is approximate and not an official plan. Do not treat unverified room, floor, route, or hostel data as authoritative.

## 8. Next improvements

1. Replace the illustration with an approved/verified campus map.
2. Add floor plans and room search after obtaining permission and checking accuracy.
3. Add “from here” starting-point selection.
4. Add accessible routes and step-free entrance details after verification.
5. Consider an installable mobile version only after students confirm they need it.

## Demo flow

1. Explain the problem using an interview finding (with permission and no identifying details).
2. Ask the audience to search for a place.
3. Show the detail card and direction clue.
4. Explain how you collected and checked location data.
5. Share what users found confusing and what you changed.
6. State the limitations and next step clearly.
